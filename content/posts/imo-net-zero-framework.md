The shipping industry has spent the last several years preparing for decarbonisation. We have improved vessel efficiency, invested in digital optimisation, reduced unnecessary fuel consumption and begun evaluating alternative fuels and new propulsion technologies.

But the next phase is different.

The IMO Net-Zero Framework has the potential to move decarbonisation from being primarily an environmental and technical agenda to becoming a core commercial consideration in how we operate, invest in and manage our fleets.

That distinction matters.

For a ship manager or fleet operator, the question is no longer simply:

"How much fuel does my vessel consume?"

Increasingly, it will become:

"What is the greenhouse-gas intensity of the energy my vessel consumes, what will that mean for compliance, and what is the total cost of operating the vessel?"

That is a much more complex question—and potentially a much more consequential one.

## First, where do we actually stand?

There is an important point that needs to be made at the outset.

The IMO Net-Zero Framework is not yet a final, legally effective regulation.

The Marine Environment Protection Committee (MEPC) approved the draft framework at MEPC 83 in April 2025. The framework was then expected to move towards formal adoption in October 2025. However, the extraordinary MEPC session in October 2025 was adjourned for one year.

The process has continued during 2026.

At MEPC 84, held from 27 April to 1 May 2026, IMO Member States agreed to establish an intersessional working group to address outstanding concerns and seek greater convergence. Two further meetings are scheduled for 1–4 September and 23–27 November 2026, followed by MEPC 85 from 30 November to 3 December 2026. The adjourned extraordinary session is expected to resume on 4 December, subject to confirmation.

So, as an industry, we should avoid two extremes.

We should not assume that every detail currently in the draft will remain unchanged.

But equally, we should not wait for every detail to be final before preparing.

The direction of travel is already clear.

## What is fundamentally changing?

The proposed framework combines two elements:

- A global fuel standard, and
- an economic mechanism linked to greenhouse-gas emissions.

The first regulates the greenhouse-gas intensity of the energy used by ships. The second puts an economic consequence on emissions above the applicable thresholds and creates incentives for zero- and near-zero-emission solutions.

This is significant because shipping's decarbonisation journey is moving beyond simply measuring the efficiency of the vessel.

The proposed framework introduces GHG Fuel Intensity, or GFI, measured in grams of CO₂ equivalent per megajoule.

More importantly, this is based on a well-to-wake perspective.

That means we are no longer looking only at what happens when fuel is burned onboard. We increasingly have to understand what happened before the fuel reached the ship. Where was it produced? What energy was used to produce it? How was it transported? What were the upstream emissions? How was the fuel certified? And can those emissions be reliably traced and verified?

These questions may sound like matters for fuel producers and regulators. They are not. They will increasingly become matters for ship operators as well.

## The fuel tank is no longer the beginning of the story

For many years, fuel management in shipping has been relatively straightforward. We look at:

Fuel price → consumption → voyage cost

The future model will be more complicated:

Fuel price + fuel lifecycle emissions + vessel efficiency + regulatory exposure + compliance cost

Two fuels that look similar from an engine-room perspective may therefore have very different regulatory and commercial outcomes. This is one of the most important consequences of the well-to-wake approach.

The carbon intensity of a fuel will depend not only on its chemical composition but also on how it is produced. That makes fuel provenance and certification strategically important.

The IMO is working on the supporting framework for fuel certification, GFI calculation and verification, and the GFI Registry. Work is also continuing on chain-of-custody models, which are intended to ensure that the origin and movement of fuels can be traced and their emissions properly accounted for.

For ship operators, this means that the relationship with the bunker supplier may eventually need to go beyond price, availability and specification. The question may increasingly be:

"What is the certified GHG intensity of the fuel we are buying?"

## Efficiency still matters—but efficiency alone will not be enough

There is sometimes a tendency to view alternative fuels as the solution and vessel efficiency as yesterday's agenda. I do not believe that is correct.

A more efficient ship will remain a competitive advantage. Every tonne of fuel that we do not consume is a tonne that we do not have to purchase, transport, handle or ultimately decarbonise.

The difference is that efficiency will increasingly work alongside fuel choice. A ship consuming less energy with a conventional fuel may have a different compliance position from a ship using a lower-GHG-intensity fuel.

This means the future competitive equation is likely to be:

Energy efficiency × fuel GHG intensity × operational performance × compliance economics

This is where good ship management becomes increasingly important. Engine performance, hull condition, propeller efficiency, weather routing, speed optimisation, trim optimisation, maintenance quality and voyage execution will continue to matter.

Decarbonisation does not make operational excellence less important. It makes it more valuable.

## A new economic layer is coming into fleet management

Under the draft framework, ships would have a Base Target and a more stringent Direct Compliance Target.

Ships performing above the relevant thresholds could face a deficit, while ships performing better could generate surplus units. Deficits could be addressed through surplus units or remedial units linked to contributions to the proposed IMO Net-Zero Fund.

The significance of this is easy to underestimate. Historically, fleet performance has largely been managed through fuel consumption, freight revenue, operating costs, maintenance costs, off-hire and vessel utilisation.

We may now have another balance sheet to manage: the fleet's GHG compliance position.

Imagine a fleet where one group of vessels consistently performs better than the regulatory requirement while another group struggles to meet it. The question will no longer be only: which vessel is consuming more fuel? It may become: where within the fleet can we generate, preserve or use compliance value most efficiently?

That creates a new optimisation problem for fleet managers.

## The IMO Net-Zero Fund changes the economics further

The proposed economic mechanism is designed to generate revenue through emissions-related contributions. The proposed IMO Net-Zero Fund would then support areas including low-emission technologies, innovation, infrastructure, training, technology transfer and measures intended to mitigate impacts on vulnerable developing states.

For shipowners, however, the immediate commercial question is simpler: what will decarbonisation cost my fleet?

That cost will not be determined by fuel price alone. It could include the premium for lower-GHG fuels; investment in fuel-ready vessels; retrofits; efficiency technologies; compliance-unit requirements; verification and certification; data management; additional administrative processes; infrastructure constraints; and potentially the cost of carrying or financing regulatory exposure.

This is why decarbonisation planning needs to move into the finance and commercial functions of a shipping company. It is no longer something that can sit exclusively with the sustainability team.

## The difficult question: what fuel do we bet on?

This may ultimately be the most difficult strategic decision facing shipowners.

There is no single fuel answer for the entire industry. The answer will depend on vessel type, trading pattern, vessel age, fuel availability, bunkering infrastructure, cargo requirements, capital cost and the lifecycle emissions of the fuel.

Methanol, ammonia, hydrogen, biofuels and other pathways each have different characteristics and different levels of maturity. The challenge is therefore not to predict with certainty which fuel will "win". The challenge is to build a fleet that can remain competitive while the answer is still evolving.

That requires flexibility. For a new vessel, fuel flexibility may become an important part of the investment decision. For an existing vessel, the question may be whether an efficiency retrofit, alternative-fuel conversion or simply continued operation is economically justified. For an older vessel, regulatory exposure may influence the point at which it becomes more economical to retire the asset rather than invest further.

This is where decarbonisation and fleet renewal become closely connected.

## The existing fleet cannot be ignored

It is easy to focus on newbuilds when discussing the energy transition. But the existing fleet will be with us for many years.

A vessel entering service today could potentially operate well into the period in which increasingly stringent GHG requirements apply. Therefore, every major vessel investment should be evaluated against its full economic life, not simply today's fuel economics.

A useful question for management teams is: if we operate this vessel for the next 15 years, what will its fuel, efficiency and compliance profile look like—not just in 2027, but in 2035 and beyond?

That changes the investment conversation. A retrofit that looks marginal when evaluated only against today's fuel price may become attractive when the avoided fuel consumption and future compliance exposure are considered together. Similarly, an apparently attractive newbuild may prove less competitive if it locks the company into a fuel pathway that becomes increasingly expensive or difficult to certify.

## CII is not going away

Another important point is that the Net-Zero Framework should not be viewed as a replacement for CII. They address different dimensions of the problem.

CII is fundamentally concerned with the operational carbon intensity of the ship. The Net-Zero Framework introduces a stronger focus on the GHG intensity of the energy used, using a lifecycle approach.

In practical terms, operators will therefore need to manage both how efficiently the vessel operates and how clean the energy used by the vessel is. A vessel that performs well on one metric but poorly on the other may not necessarily be in the strongest position.

This is why decarbonisation should increasingly be managed as an integrated fleet-performance programme rather than as a collection of separate regulatory projects.

## Data will become a competitive capability

There is another change that may receive less attention than alternative fuels, but I believe it will be equally important: data quality.

If GFI is going to influence compliance and economics, then the underlying data has to be accurate — fuel consumption data, fuel quality data, fuel origin, lifecycle emissions, bunkering records, voyage information, engine performance, cargo carried, operational conditions and verification records. All of these will increasingly need to connect.

This means that the digital infrastructure supporting fleet management will become part of the decarbonisation strategy. The companies that can reliably connect fuel data → vessel performance → GHG intensity → compliance exposure → financial impact will be better positioned to make decisions quickly.

The value will not come from collecting more data. It will come from turning data into decisions.

## What should ship managers do now?

The regulatory outcome is still being negotiated. That does not mean management should wait. I would focus on five areas.

1. **Establish the baseline.** Know the current GHG performance of every vessel — not just fleet averages, but vessel by vessel, route by route, fuel by fuel.
2. **Build a forward-looking GFI model.** We should be able to answer: what happens to our fleet's GFI under different fuel and operating scenarios? That model should be flexible enough to accommodate changes in the final regulation.
3. **Protect operational efficiency.** The cheapest tonne of CO₂ to eliminate is often the one we never create. Hull performance, engine condition, voyage optimisation and speed management remain fundamental.
4. **Develop a fuel strategy—not a fuel prediction.** We do not need to know today which fuel will dominate in 2040. We need to understand the economics and risks of the plausible pathways and ensure that our fleet investment decisions preserve optionality.
5. **Put a financial value on carbon performance.** Decarbonisation decisions should increasingly be evaluated using a common financial language, for example: fuel saving + avoided compliance exposure + potential value of surplus performance − capital investment − additional operating cost = economic value of the decarbonisation measure.

This is how decarbonisation becomes part of mainstream fleet management.

## The real challenge is not technology

Technology will continue to develop. The bigger challenge, in my view, is coordination.

The future fuel system requires shipowners, charterers, fuel producers, ports, infrastructure providers, classification societies, regulators, financiers and technology companies to move in the same direction.

A ship cannot operate on a zero- or near-zero-emission fuel if the fuel is not available. A fuel producer will not make large investments without confidence that there will be demand. A port will not build infrastructure without confidence that ships will call. A shipowner will not order vessels without confidence in fuel availability and economics.

This is the classic chicken-and-egg problem of energy transition. The IMO framework is important partly because a global regulatory signal can help create the demand needed to unlock that investment. Whether the final framework achieves that objective will depend heavily on the details that are still being negotiated.

## What I believe shipping companies should avoid

There are three traps we should avoid.

The first is waiting for certainty. The final regulatory details may change. But the direction is clear enough to start preparing.

The second is choosing a technology too early. We should avoid making large irreversible bets based on assumptions about a fuel market that is still developing.

The third is treating compliance as the objective. Compliance is the minimum requirement. The real objective should be to build a fleet that remains safe, efficient, commercially competitive and environmentally credible as the regulatory environment changes.

## The next two years will be important

The September and November 2026 intersessional discussions, followed by MEPC 85, will be particularly important in determining how the outstanding issues around the framework develop. IMO has specifically identified areas including implementation guidelines, fuel certification, the GFI calculation and compliance approach, ZNZ fuels and rewards, the Net-Zero Fund and the GFI Registry as areas requiring further work.

There is still uncertainty. But uncertainty should not be confused with inactivity. The responsible approach for a shipping company is to prepare for the direction of regulation, while remaining flexible on the details.

## From compliance to competitive advantage

Ultimately, I do not believe the IMO Net-Zero Framework should be viewed simply as another regulatory burden. It is a structural change in the economics of shipping.

For decades, competitive advantage in shipping has largely been built around: cost per day + fuel efficiency + utilisation + network + commercial execution.

The next generation of competitive advantage will increasingly include: energy efficiency + fuel lifecycle performance + data quality + regulatory readiness + fleet flexibility.

That is a significant change. The companies that respond best will not necessarily be those that make the biggest announcement about decarbonisation. They will be the companies that quietly build the capability to answer, vessel by vessel:

- What are we consuming?
- What is its real GHG intensity?
- What will regulation cost us?
- Where can we improve?
- Where should we invest?
- And how do we remain competitive while the energy transition unfolds?

The IMO Net-Zero Framework is still being shaped. But from an operator's perspective, the message is already clear.

Decarbonisation is moving from a target on the horizon to an operating variable in the business. And the companies that start treating it that way today will be better prepared for whatever the final framework looks like tomorrow.
