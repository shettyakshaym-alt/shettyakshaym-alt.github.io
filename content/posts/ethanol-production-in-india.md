## A systems view of India’s ethanol economy

This note looks at ethanol not as a side product, but as a national energy system: how much feedstock capacity exists, how much is actually absorbed, what happens to exports, and what changes when blending rises further.

> Figures below are rounded and should be read as public-estimate ranges. Exact values move by season, feedstock mix, and reporting window.

## 1) How much raw material capacity does India have?

India’s ethanol ecosystem is built on three feedstock pillars:

| Feedstock route | Typical inputs | Role in the system |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar route | Sugarcane juice, B-heavy/C-heavy molasses | Legacy backbone of Indian ethanol |
| Grain route | Maize, broken rice, damaged food grains | Fastest-growing capacity addition |
| 2G / lignocellulosic | Agri-residue, bagasse, biomass | Strategic long-term hedge |

The **installed / approvable distillation capacity** across these routes is now broadly in the **1,700-1,900 crore litre/year** range, though actual effective capacity is lower because of:

- seasonal feedstock availability,
- plant utilization,
- working capital cycles,
- transport and blending logistics,
- and feedstock allocation across sugar, ethanol, and other end uses.

In other words, India has built more ethanol capacity than it can always fully use in a single season.

## 2) How much do we actually produce?

The best public proxy for domestic ethanol absorption is **ethanol supplied to OMCs** for blending.

- In **ESY 2023-24**, India supplied roughly **707 crore litres** of ethanol to oil marketing companies.
- That number matters because it reflects the real blending sink, not just installed plant capacity.

The key analytical distinction is:

1. **Installed capacity** = what India can theoretically make  
2. **Actual production** = what distilleries physically turn out  
3. **OMC offtake / blending supply** = what is finally consumed in the fuel pool

For policy analysis, #3 is the number that drives import substitution, sugar balance, and gasoline displacement.

## 3) What about exports?

India is no longer an export-led ethanol market.

As domestic blending has accelerated, the home market has become the dominant absorber. Export volumes are therefore **small and policy-sensitive** relative to domestic use. In practical terms:

- exports are **not** the core growth engine,
- they are a **residual outlet** after domestic allocation,
- and they tend to rise only when domestic blending does not fully absorb available supply.

So the correct macro conclusion is not "India exports ethanol at scale"; it is:

**India is building ethanol as a domestic decarbonization asset, not as a commodity export story.**

## 4) What changed after ethanol blending took off?

The 20% blending program changed the industry structure in five important ways:

### a) Sugar mills became energy platforms
Sugar mills are no longer just sugar mills. They are increasingly integrated into:

- ethanol,
- cogeneration,
- and co-product monetization.

### b) Cane economics improved
Diverting cane juice / B-heavy molasses into ethanol:

- reduces sugar surplus pressure,
- improves mill cash flow,
- and helps stabilize cane payment cycles.

### c) Grain markets gained an industrial buyer
Maize and broken rice now have a stronger offtake channel, which has:

- expanded grain-based distillery economics,
- created a new industrial demand floor,
- and influenced acreage decisions in some states.

### d) Oil import exposure fell
Every litre of ethanol blended into petrol displaces a litre of gasoline-equivalent demand, cutting:

- crude import dependence,
- foreign exchange outflow,
- and carbon intensity.

### e) The trade-off became clearer
Higher blending is not free. It introduces pressure on:

- crop allocation,
- water use,
- logistics,
- and life-cycle emissions accounting.

## 5) What if India goes above 20% blending?

Let the petrol pool be **P** and the blending rate be **b**.

Then ethanol demand is:

**E = b × P**

If 20% blending corresponds to roughly **1,016 crore litres** of ethanol demand, then:

- **25% blending** implies about **1,270 crore litres**
- incremental need is roughly **250 crore litres/year**

That incremental demand is large enough to matter structurally. It would likely be met through some combination of:

- more cane juice / molasses diversion,
- more grain-based ethanol,
- better plant utilization,
- and faster 2G deployment.

### Sensitivity
At the current petrol pool size, **every 1 percentage point increase in blending adds roughly 50 crore litres of ethanol demand**.

So moving from 20% to 25% is not a cosmetic policy shift — it is a major industrial expansion.

## 6) State-wise overview

India’s ethanol geography is uneven. The market is clustered around feedstock and processing ecosystems.

| State / cluster | Main feedstock base | Strategic role |
|---|---|---|
| Uttar Pradesh | Cane + grain | One of the largest integrated ethanol states |
| Maharashtra | Cane / molasses | Core sugar-ethanol belt |
| Karnataka | Cane / molasses | Strong sugar mill integration |
| Tamil Nadu | Cane + grain | Mixed feedstock and industrial capacity |
| Bihar | Grain | High-growth grain-based cluster |
| Punjab / Haryana | Grain | Maize and surplus grain based potential |
| Andhra Pradesh / Telangana | Grain | Fast-growing grain-to-ethanol corridor |
| Gujarat | Mixed | Diversified industrial capacity |
| Madhya Pradesh / Chhattisgarh | Grain + biomass | Emerging balance of grain and residue potential |

### What this map means
- **Sugar states** carry the historical backbone.
- **Grain states** are now the fastest-expanding frontier.
- **2G capacity** is the long-term stabilizer if India wants higher blending without excessive food-fuel tension.

## 7) The real policy question

The question is not whether India can technically make more ethanol.

It is whether India can increase blending while keeping:

- food price pressure manageable,
- water use acceptable,
- emissions genuinely lower on a life-cycle basis,
- and feedstock markets stable.

That is why the next phase must be guided by:

1. feedstock productivity,
2. 2G commercialization,
3. logistics planning,
4. state-level capacity mapping,
5. and rigorous lifecycle carbon accounting.

## Bottom line

India’s ethanol program has already moved beyond pilot economics. It is now a large-scale energy transition instrument.

The next frontier is not just "more ethanol" — it is **more ethanol with better feedstock efficiency, stronger state-wise planning, and lower lifecycle carbon intensity**.
